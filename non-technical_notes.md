# What we are learning about how a large language model handles new words

*A non-technical summary for cross-disciplinary readers. Updated May 2026 after the lexical-identity control.*

## The bottom line, in four sentences

We are testing whether large language models (the family of systems that includes ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini) actually internalise the abstract structure of what they read, or whether they are very sophisticated pattern-matchers operating over surface text. What we have found, after nine months of progressively harder tests, is sharper than either of those framings: the model *does* build an abstract, substrate-independent representation of *the specific words it has been trained on* — its internal geometry of `and`, `or`, `not`, `implies`, `necessarily` survives wholesale changes in surface presentation (English prose vs programming-style function calls), and (a recent pre-registered control we report below) so does its internal geometry of any other word it knows, including 15 ordinary content words like `house`, `water`, and `archipelago` placed in the same syntactic positions. The substrate-invariance is a property of *anything in the trained vocabulary*, not specifically of logical-operator-class abstractions. But this abstract structure *does not extend to novel words*. When the model is shown a made-up word in the position where a logical operator should go, it does not figure out what kind of operator the new word is; it routes the new word to whichever familiar operator happens to be the model's "low-confidence default", and that default shifts as we change the set of operators the model is allowed to choose from. The model has a Platonic-like geometry for its trained vocabulary; it lacks a Platonic-like *category* of "logical operator" that would generalise to new instances.

The rest of this note explains the test that produced this finding, the three rounds of progressive falsification that tightened it, and why the result sits in an empirically interesting middle ground between the two common framings of large language models.

---

## The big question

When a large language model writes something that *looks* like logical reasoning — "If P and Q, then P, so this conclusion follows" — is it doing anything that resembles logical reasoning internally? Or is it doing a kind of advanced autocomplete over the text patterns it has seen in training?

This question is harder than it sounds, because the model's *output* gives us almost no traction on the answer. A parrot can produce the words "the bird is on the table" without anything corresponding to a thought about a bird, a table, or aboveness. Asking the model "do you understand this?" returns "yes" for the same uninformative reason. The only way to make progress is to look inside the model — at the patterns of numerical activity that arise as it processes a sentence — and ask whether those internal patterns reflect the underlying structure or only the surface text.

There is a strong hypothesis in the field — sometimes called the *Platonic Representation Hypothesis* — that says: yes, large enough neural networks do converge on representations of underlying structure, in the same way that mathematicians converge on "the integers" regardless of whether they write them as `1, 2, 3` or `I, II, III` or `一, 二, 三`. If true, this would have major consequences for how we think about machine cognition, and also for what we should expect these systems to do when faced with genuinely new domains.

We are testing this hypothesis sharply, and the result is more constrained and more interesting than either "yes" or "no".

## Our test, in plain language

We use logical sentences — short propositions involving the connectives *and*, *or*, *not*, *implies*, *necessarily* — as our raw material. These connectives have very small, very well-defined meanings, and they let us build sentences whose structure we can specify exactly.

Then we make two kinds of changes that should not affect the structure:

1. **We change the variable letters.** A sentence written with English letters (P, Q, R) gets rewritten with Greek letters (α, β, γ). The structure is identical; only the symbols differ.

2. **We change the operator words.** We replace *and*, *or*, *not*, *implies*, *necessarily* with completely made-up words — *bliq*, *dren*, *vusp*, *molex*, *perph*. The model has never seen these as logical operators. From a structural point of view, the sentence is unchanged; from a surface point of view, the connecting words are unrecognisable.

3. **We change the notation.** We write the same logical content in two completely different formats: English metalinguistic frames ("Consider the word *and* in this sentence") and programming-style function calls ("The function and(p, q) evaluates to true or false"). The two formats use the same operators but in syntactically very different positions.

Then we ask: does the model's internal representation of the modified sentence still resemble the representation of the original?

## What happens with variables: business as usual

For the variable change (English letters → Greek letters), the model treats the two versions as essentially the same. A classifier trained to recognise "which operator is in this sentence" by looking at the model's internals achieves perfect accuracy whether the variables are English or Greek. The model has fully abstracted the role of "variable" away from the surface symbol. This is the Platonic-hypothesis result you would hope for, and it is consistent with the model genuinely encoding the structural slot rather than the surface character.

## What happens with familiar operators across notation: also Platonic, also clean

This is the genuinely new finding from this year, and it deserves to be stated cleanly. **The model's internal representation of the five familiar logical operators — `and`, `or`, `not`, `implies`, `necessarily` — is the same across the English-prose and programming-style notation.** A classifier trained on the model's representations of operators in one notation correctly identifies operators in the other notation, at roughly eight to ten times chance accuracy. The model has learned that `and` in `Consider the word and in the sentence` is the *same* `and` as in `The function and(p, q) evaluates to true or false`, despite the two sentences being structurally completely different at the surface.

This survives every robustness test we have thrown at it. We added five more operators (`xor`, `nand`, `possibly`, `always`, `negate`) and re-extracted everything; the transfer still works at near-perfect accuracy. We resampled the test sentences hundreds of times to get statistical confidence intervals; the interval is tight and well above the threshold. We checked both directions (English → programming and programming → English); both directions work.

**This is the strongest evidence we have for a Platonic-like internal structure.** It says: the model's geometry of *its trained logical operators* is substrate-independent in a precise and measurable sense.

## What happens with made-up operators: the surprising story

This is where the story becomes more interesting, and where it has changed three times over the past four months as we ran progressively harder tests.

### First reading (early Phase 1, autumn 2025)

When we first ran the made-up-operator test, the finding was as we described in the previous version of this note: the model classifies every made-up word as one of the *modifier* operators (`not` or `necessarily`), and almost never as one of the *connector* operators (`and`, `or`, `implies`). Hundreds of test sentences, zero classified as `and`. Looked like a clean asymmetry: the model can place novel words into the modifier category but not the connector category.

### Second reading (after the cross-notation transfer test)

When we extended the test to cross-notation transfer — train on English-prose sentences, test on programming-style sentences and vice versa — the picture became more nuanced. There were specific layers and specific positions in the model where the apparent asymmetry seemed to *also* respect the intended distinction. In particular, at certain training positions (the model's representation of the sentence after it has seen the full function call, like the closing parenthesis or the end of the sentence), the made-up words *seemed* to land in the correct category — `bliq` was treated like a connector at four out of five tries; `vusp` was treated like a modifier consistently.

This was exciting. It looked like the model might, after all, be performing context-grounded category assignment for novel words, but only at specific positions in its information flow.

### Third reading (after the falsification tests)

Then we ran two stress tests designed to either confirm this or break it. The first added more made-up words (we expanded from 5 to 16, balanced so that 8 were intended as connectors and 8 as modifiers). The second added more real operators (we expanded the model's choices from 5 canonical operators to 10, including the rarely-used `xor`, `nand`, `possibly`, `always`, and `negate`).

The falsification tests broke the second reading.

**Made-up-word expansion (5 → 16 made-up words):** Two of the four positive cells dropped to chance-level performance. The "4 out of 5 made-up words land in the correct category" pattern that had looked exciting was substantially a small-sample artifact: when you have only 5 made-up words and the model has a default-canonical it routes everything to, the probability of *coincidentally* matching the intended categories of the made-up set is not negligible. Two cells still passed at the larger sample size — encouraging.

**Real-operator expansion (5 → 10 canonical operators):** The remaining two positive cells *also* dropped to chance, in a way that revealed the underlying mechanism. With the expanded operator set, 100% of all made-up words started getting routed to the new operator `nand` (which exists in formal logic but is essentially absent from natural English text). At the cell that had previously been our cleanest positive result — every made-up word now landed on `nand`. Every single one. Including the made-up words we had intended to be modifiers.

The mechanism we had glimpsed turned out not to be category-respecting at all. It is what we now call **default-to-rarest-canonical**: when shown an unfamiliar word in an operator position, the model routes it to whichever familiar operator sits in the *most uncertain part* of the model's decision space. With five common operators that's `necessarily` or `implies`; with ten operators where five are nearly-zero-frequency `xor` / `nand` / `possibly` / `always` / `negate`, the target shifts wholesale to those rare ones. The apparent "category respect" we had seen in the second reading was a coincidence between this default-routing behaviour and the intended distribution of the small made-up set.

### Fourth reading (after the pre-registered cross-family stress test)

We then replicated the entire programme on **Pythia 6.9B**, a third model family trained by EleutherAI on a third data source (the Pile). The two-part finding replicated cleanly: cross-notation operator transfer works perfectly on Pythia's trained operators, and novel-operator generalisation fails by the same default-routing mechanism — though Pythia routes its defaults to a slightly different mixture of rare operators than OLMo or Gemma does.

After the three-model replication landed, we ran one final **pre-registered** stress test, designed specifically to refute the default-routing reading. We wrote down the analysis plan before running the experiment, and added five new operators chosen to distinguish two possible single-factor explanations of the default mechanism: pure rarity (the operator is uncommon in training data) versus subword shape (the operator is split into multiple pieces by the tokenizer, like the made-up words are). The new operators spanned all four combinations of these two factors, including two "boring" mid-frequency operators that *neither* factor predicts should attract novel-word mass.

None of the simple single-factor predictions matched the data in any of the three models. The mid-frequency control operators stayed quiet (good — the controls held). But the predicted "winners" were not the actual winners in any model. The most striking anomaly was OLMo routing 16% of all novel-word mass to `implies` — a familiar mid-frequency operator that was already in our readout from the very first experiment and that none of our pre-registered predictions targeted.

What this means: the default-routing mechanism is more nuanced than "route to the rarest operator". It seems to depend on a model-specific mixture of rarity, subword shape, and how close each novel word is to each familiar operator in the model's internal geometry. The strong cross-notation transfer on trained operators got even stronger in this test — with the readout expanded to 15 operators, all three models still classified all 15 operators perfectly across the notation boundary, with no measurable wobble. The positive half of the finding (which at this stage we were still calling "operator-set-bound substrate-invariance"; the seventh-reading control below broadens this to "trained-vocabulary substrate-invariance") is the most secure result in the project; the negative half is real but mechanism-wise more interesting than the original "default to the rarest" framing suggested.

The pre-registration also caught one unforeseeable design problem: one of our new operators (`iff`, the biconditional symbol used in mathematical logic) was supposed to be a multi-piece token (we expected it to split into something like `'if' + 'f'`), but it turned out to be a single piece in all three tokenizers — meaning it ended up in the wrong cell of our four-way design. This is exactly the kind of thing pre-registration is supposed to catch: a flagged design failure recorded openly, rather than discovered post-hoc and silently worked around.

### Fifth reading (after the causal-intervention test, May 2026)

We then performed the causal-intervention test that had been the highest-priority outstanding experiment. The previous readings, including the pre-registered cross-family one, all relied on a passive instrument — a linear classifier reading from the model's internal state — and the strongest critique of any such instrument is that it might be reading structure that exists in the model's representations without actually driving the model's downstream behaviour. To distinguish these, we directly replaced specific activation patterns in the model during processing (substituting the model's representation of a made-up word with the model's representation of `not`, or of `and`) and measured (i) whether the linear classifier read the substituted operator (sanity check) and (ii) whether the model's actual *next-token output behaviour* shifted toward the behaviour we would expect for that substituted operator (the causal test).

The result split three ways across the three cells we tested, and is one of the most informative outcomes of the project.

At one cell in Gemma 2 9B — a specific layer and position where the pre-registered v6 test had flagged emergent category-respect behaviour — the substitution was causally real: substituting a connector activation made the model produce connector-style next-token outputs; substituting a modifier activation made it produce modifier-style outputs. The wrong-arity flip test (intended-modifier word + connector substitution → connector behaviour, and the symmetric direction) passed cleanly in both directions, every time. This cell is the first place in any of our tests where novel-operator behaviour is causally arity-respecting; it is a single, narrowly scoped exception to the negative finding (novel-operator generalisation failure), specific to Gemma 2 9B at one specific layer and position when the substitution is sourced from a specific reference location in the sentence.

At a second cell in Gemma 2 9B — same layer, same position, but with the substitution sourced from a slightly different reference position in the sentence — the substitution was *not* causally real. The linear classifier read the substituted operator perfectly (the patch reached the residual) but the model's downstream behaviour did not shift toward the substituted operator's reference. A random-noise control substitution actually produced larger behavioural shifts than the targeted substitution did. This second cell is therefore confirmed as a measurement artifact rather than a real structural finding — exactly what the pre-registered v6 reading had treated it as.

The third cell — the strongest positive finding in OLMo 2 7B, the project's flagship cross-notation transfer result — also turned out to be probe-readable but not causally load-bearing. The classifier read the substituted operator at 100% accuracy, confirming the substitution reached the model's internal state, but the downstream behavioural shifts were flat or in the wrong direction. This does not refute the underlying finding (the linear-probe transfer at this cell remains robust and replicates across three model families at near-perfect accuracy), but it does sharpen what that finding means: the model has a *geometric* representation of cross-notation operator structure at this position that a linear classifier can read out, but that geometric structure is not on the causal path between input and output at this specific position.

Two refinements to the bottom-line picture:

- **The position from which the substitution is sourced matters more than we had appreciated.** Both Gemma cells tested are at the same layer and same target position, with identical 100% classifier-readability — they differ only in which reference position the substitute activation was taken from. The two cells give opposite causal verdicts. The standard methodology of reporting a single classifier accuracy at a single anchor pair would systematically miss this dissociation; future probe-based studies of compositional structure should include same-target / different-source causal tests as part of the standard battery.

- **The strongest positive finding in the project has a causal nuance.** Cross-notation operator structure on trained operators is a real geometric property of mid-scale base language models — but it is not uniformly causally load-bearing across all positions where the linear probe can read it. This is consistent with the model having multiple internal pathways through which the same information can flow, only some of which are downstream-relevant at any given position. A more comprehensive map of this causal landscape is the natural next step. The headline picture is unchanged at this stage — *trained-operator substrate-invariance is real, and the novel-operator generalisation failure is real* (the seventh-reading control will then broaden the positive half to trained-vocabulary substrate-invariance) — but we now know that the trained-operator structure is causally load-bearing at some positions and geometrically-present-but-causally-inert at others, and we have one tightly scoped causal exception in Gemma 2 9B where a novel-operator behaviour is genuinely arity-respecting under intervention.

### Sixth reading (after the embedding-similarity test, May 2026)

The final outstanding question from the pre-registered fourth-reading test was *which* familiar operator each novel word gets routed to, given that no single-factor mechanism (raw rarity, raw subword shape, or the combination) predicts the data. The most natural remaining hypothesis was *contextual semantic neighborhood*: each novel word's default attractor should be the familiar operator that the novel word sits closest to in the model's internal geometry, controlled for connector-vs-modifier role. We tested this directly across all three model families with a simple geometric measurement (cosine similarity between the average internal representation of a novel word and the average internal representation of each familiar operator, at the same layer and position the classifier reads from).

The hypothesis fails cleanly across all three models. Once we ask whether geometric proximity predicts which specific familiar operator the classifier routes a given novel word to, agreement is at the random-guess baseline. The geometric heuristic does capture connector-vs-modifier role moderately well (it gets the role right about 60-66% of the time in two of three models, vs ~53% chance baseline), but it cannot distinguish between operators *within* a role. At the methodologically-interesting positions — where the classifier spreads its predictions across several familiar operators rather than collapsing everything to a single attractor — the geometric heuristic identifies the correct operator only 11-16% of the time, barely above the 7% chance baseline.

The cleanest failure signature: across all three models at distributed routing positions, the geometric argmax collapses every novel word to a single familiar operator (`nand`, the formal-logic NAND symbol that is essentially absent from natural text). This is most likely a geometric artifact rather than a semantic finding — `nand`'s average internal representation is unusually far from any sensible operator centroid because it has the most idiosyncratic per-stimulus geometry of the operators we tested, so under cosine similarity novel words (also far from any sensible centroid) end up closest to it. The classifier, which uses learned weights rather than raw geometric distance, is robust to this artifact because it is optimised to discriminate between operators rather than to find the nearest centroid.

Two refinements to the bottom-line picture:

- **The residual mechanism is something we are calling *probe-decision-boundary geometry*.** The classifier captures per-word internal-state structure that the simple geometric heuristic destroys when it averages. This is a substantive distinction — it tells us the model's per-novel-word routing is *not* a simple "closest familiar operator" lookup but rather involves a more sophisticated discriminative signal that we now know is present in the model's residual stream but cannot be characterised with a single mean-pooled cosine statistic. Identifying what feature of the residual stream the classifier is reading is a follow-up question (most naturally addressed with sparse-autoencoder feature labelling) and is not blocking the paper.

- **The categorical structure is built during processing, not inherited from how the model represents the novel words at its input.** At the embedding layer — the model's first internal representation of the novel words, before any processing — the geometric heuristic's agreement with the classifier drops to 0-2% across all three models. The connector-vs-modifier categorical structure is constructed by the model's mid-depth processing layers; it does not exist at the input. This is a clean cross-family re-confirmation of an earlier finding (from a 2025 experiment on a single model) under the much larger operator vocabulary of the current stress-test.

### Seventh reading (after the lexical-identity control, May 2026)

The first draft of the paper went out for external review by a paper-writing expert who is also a machine-learning expert. The principal scientific concern in that review was sharp: the "operator-set-bound substrate-invariance" framing might be overclaimed. The model's cross-notation transfer is at perfect accuracy on 15 trained logical operators, yes — but the same instrument might *also* return perfect accuracy on 15 arbitrary content words like `house`, `water`, `archipelago` if you inserted them into syntactically-identical templates. If so, the result is not about logical-operator-class structure at all; it is about the more generic property "any well-tokenized in-vocabulary word transfers its identity across notation changes", and the interesting positive finding is correspondingly broader and shallower than we had been describing.

The reviewer recommended a single pre-registered control: re-run the cross-notation transfer test with 15 non-operator content words substituted into the same templates the canonical operators occupy. If transfer is below threshold on content words, the operator-class framing is intact; if transfer is at or near ceiling on content words, we are looking at a generic lexical-identity property and the paper needs to be reframed.

We ran the control and the reframe trigger fired in all three models. At the cell that anchors the project's principal positive finding (`operator-after L 4` in the N→F direction), cross-notation transfer on the 15 content words reaches perfect accuracy — bootstrap 95% CI [1.000, 1.000] — exactly like the 15 trained logical operators. Two of the 15 content words tokenise into 2-3 subword pieces (matching the canonical set's 1/15 multi-piece rate), and the within-condition probe sanity check is also at ceiling. The transfer is byte-identical to the canonical-operator result.

This sharpens the bottom-line picture rather than overturning it. The model has a substrate-independent geometry for *the words it was trained on at the operator-after position under cross-notation rewriting*, and this property is not specific to logical-operator-class semantics — it generalises to any in-vocabulary word the model knows. The negative finding (novel-operator generalisation fails) is preserved as stated: we have only shown the failure for *novel operator* words; whether novel content words would also fail to generalise (rather than transfer at ceiling like in-vocabulary content words do) is an open follow-up. The reframed positive finding is simultaneously broader (any in-vocab word, not just operators) and shallower (lexical-identity substrate-invariance, not substrate-invariant logical-operator semantics) than the v1-draft framing — but it is the more accurate fit to the data.

The reframe itself is a methodological contribution. The pre-registered within-vocabulary content-word control is a directly transferable diagnostic that any probe-based substrate-invariance study should include before claiming domain-specific representation. Without it, the v1 draft would have been published with an "operator-set-bound" headline that the data does not actually support.

One small model-specific anomaly surfaced in the control. In the reverse direction (train probe on programming-style content words, test on English-prose content words), OLMo's transfer drops to 0.376 while Gemma and Pythia stay above 0.83. This is below the pre-registered threshold for that model in the reverse direction only; it does not appear at the same cell with the trained logical operators, and we document it as a methodological caveat on reverse-direction robustness for content-word probes in OLMo. It does not affect the principal-direction REFRAME trigger.

With this seventh reading, the experimental programme is closed. The paper draft is being rewritten around the broader "trained-vocabulary substrate-invariance" framing. Future work will identify what specific feature of the model's residual stream the classifier is reading (the open question from the sixth reading) and extend the substrate-invariance test to non-propositional structural domains (set theory, simple type theory, finite-group arithmetic).

## What the finding actually tells us

The cleanest synthesis of all seven readings is something we are calling **trained-vocabulary substrate-invariance**, and it has a precise meaning:

**Inside the model's trained vocabulary**: the model has substrate-independent geometric structure. It knows that `and` is `and` whether it appears in English prose or in a function call. It can distinguish all 15 operators in our test at near-perfect accuracy after a single notation change. The seventh-reading content-word control shows that this is not specific to logical operators — `house` is also `house` across the same notation change, at the same accuracy. The model has a genuinely abstract representation of *the words it was trained on*, at the relevant internal position, under cross-notation rewriting. This part of the data supports the Platonic Representation Hypothesis, in a restricted form.

**Outside the trained operator set**: the model has no equivalent abstract category. It cannot take a never-before-seen word and place it in the logical-operator framework in a way that respects the word's intended role. Instead, novel words are routed by a low-confidence default that responds to which familiar operators are nearby — a *compressive* behaviour, not a structural one. (We have only demonstrated this failure for novel operator words; whether novel content words would exhibit the same pattern is an open follow-up.)

The two facts together locate a specific *edge*: the model's Platonic-like structure stops at the boundary of its trained vocabulary. The model has not abstracted "logical-operator-ness" as a category that extends to new instances; it has substrate-invariant geometry for the specific words it has memorised, and that geometry generalises substrate-invariantly across surface-notation changes but not across word-identity changes.

This is more constrained than either of the two extreme positions ("they're just autocomplete" / "they encode the underlying structure") and we think it's the more interesting middle ground.

A note on naming. An earlier draft of the paper called this finding "operator-set-bound substrate-invariance", framing it as a property specifically of the model's trained *operator* set. The seventh-reading content-word control (above) showed that the operator-class restriction was not in the data — any in-vocabulary word transfers substrate-invariantly in the same way. The current framing is correspondingly broader and shallower: the substrate-invariance is a lexical-identity property of the trained vocabulary, not a category-membership property of logical operators specifically.

## Why we are confident in the finding

We have been actively trying to break this result for months, and the falsifications we have run are themselves the principal evidence:

- It appears in models from **three different families**: OLMo 2 (from the Allen Institute) at the 7-billion-parameter scale, Gemma 2 (from Google) at the 9-billion-parameter scale, and Pythia (from EleutherAI) at the 6.9-billion-parameter scale. Three different research labs, three different training data sources (Dolma, Google proprietary, the Pile), three different model architectures, and three different ways of splitting words into pieces.

- The cross-notation canonical-operator transfer (the Platonic-supporting half of the finding) survives **every robustness test we have run**: bootstrap resampling of test sentences, expansion of the operator set from 5 to 10 to 15 canonical operators, multiple anchor positions in the sentence, both directions of transfer, and now a pre-registered fourth stress test with five new operators chosen specifically to probe the mechanism. At the 15-operator readout the model still classifies all 15 operators perfectly when the probe is trained in one notation and tested in the other — every single time, in every single model.

- The novel-operator generalisation failure (the compression-favouring half) was actually *positive* in two intermediate stages of our analysis. Both times the apparent positive result was retracted by a subsequent falsification test. The retractions revealed the underlying mechanism. We think this is the strongest possible evidence that the negative result is real: we set up tests that *could* have refuted it and they did not.

- The latest stress test was **pre-registered** — we wrote down the analysis plan and the predictions before running the experiment, to address a methodological concern that our earlier readings had been refined iteratively on the same data (a known source of research-design fragility). The pre-registered predictions about *which* familiar operator the model should default novel words to all failed in all three models, which itself is informative: the default mechanism is **not** a clean single-factor process (e.g. "route to the rarest operator"), but rather a model-specific mixture of how rare an operator is in training data, how it gets split into subword pieces by the tokenizer, and how close it is to the novel word in the model's internal geometry. The pre-registration also caught one design flaw we couldn't have foreseen — one of our new operators (`iff`, the biconditional) ended up being a single subword in all three tokenizers despite us expecting it to be multiple subwords — which is exactly what pre-registration is for: catching design errors before they contaminate confirmatory readings of the data.

- The categorical distinction the model makes between connector and modifier operators (the strong Phase 0 finding) is built **inside the model** during processing — it is not present at the input layer where the model first sees the words. We can show, by looking at the model's input-layer representations, that the made-up words start their journey through the network looking nothing like the operators they end up classified as. The category structure is constructed by the model's intermediate computation, not inherited from word appearance.

## Why this matters

Three concrete stakes for non-academic readers and readers in adjacent fields.

**For artificial intelligence — compositional generalisation.** A long-standing target for AI systems is the ability to take familiar building blocks and recombine them in genuinely new ways, including with novel components. Our finding is a precise local report on how the current generation of large language models handles one specific version of this: when handed a new logical operator, the model does not figure out from context what kind of operator the new symbol is. It routes the new symbol to whichever familiar operator sits in its low-confidence default region — a position that *depends on which other operators are in the model's vocabulary*. This is important for any application that relies on a model handling truly novel symbols (new technical vocabulary, new domain-specific operators, new procedural primitives), where the user might assume the model is inferring the symbol's role from context. It is not. It is performing a kind of soft-look-up against its trained vocabulary, with the look-up target shifting as the vocabulary changes.

**For cognitive science — comparison with human learners.** Human language users handle novel words flexibly: a child shown one example of a new connector can use it as a connector, and the same child shown one example of a new modifier can use it as a modifier. Our model exhibits a clean failure of this capacity at a specific edge — it has the trained-operator categories but cannot induct new instances into them. Whether this is a feature of the specific architecture, the training data, or the scale we tested at is an open empirical question. It maps cleanly onto known asymmetries in human category learning, and we suspect that cognitive scientists will have informed opinions about whether the model's specific failure resembles any known pattern in human or non-human animal learning.

**For the philosophy of AI capability claims.** Public discussion of large language models often oscillates between "they understand language" and "they are just sophisticated parrots". Findings like ours suggest that the truth is finer-grained than either pole, and that the genuinely interesting empirical territory is in characterising *exactly which kinds* of abstract structure the models have and have not built. If the trained-vocabulary-bound pattern we describe generalises across more models, it gives a concrete handle on this debate: "the systems represent the words they were trained on substrate-invariantly across notation changes, but do not generalise that property to novel operators by inferring their role from context" is a substantive statement that is neither dismissive nor inflationary.

## How the research evolved (a brief methodological note)

It is worth noting that our headline retracted three times over the course of this work, and we think this is good news rather than embarrassing. Each retraction revealed a more accurate picture:

- The first reading suggested a clean modifier-vs-connector asymmetry. We tested it across multiple notations and at multiple positions in the model. At specific positions, we *appeared* to find a stronger result: the asymmetry was conditional, and at certain positions the model seemed to perform context-grounded category assignment for novel words.

- The second reading — context-grounded category assignment — survived several initial robustness checks (bootstrap resampling, broader testing). But it did not survive the two stimulus-expansion stress tests we designed specifically to break it.

- The third reading — operator-set-bound substrate-invariance — was what the v1 paper draft reported as the headline positive finding. It was supported by all the experimental tests we ran on it, but was retracted under external paper review: the reviewer correctly noted that we had not run the within-vocabulary control that would distinguish "logical-operator-class substrate-invariance" from "trained-vocabulary substrate-invariance".

- The fourth reading — trained-vocabulary substrate-invariance — is what we are now reporting. It is the seventh-reading content-word control's positive result, and it generalises the v1-draft positive finding (operators-only) to any word in the model's vocabulary.

The methodology has now stabilised. Future work in this area should pre-register the within-vocabulary content-word control alongside the stimulus-expansion tests as required checks, not optional ones.

## Cross-disciplinary hooks

This is the part where we would most welcome outside input. Three of these questions are unchanged from the earlier version of these notes; one is new.

- **Linguistics and child language acquisition.** Children develop syntactic categories early and can place new words into them from limited context. Is the trained-vocabulary-bound pattern we see in the model — substrate-invariant representations for trained instances at any word class, combined with an inability to induct novel members into a target operator class — anything like documented stages of human acquisition? Open-class versus closed-class category distinctions seem like a natural comparison, but we suspect there is a more specific empirical literature we are missing.

- **Philosophy of mind, concept formation.** The model raises a precise version of an old question: when does *possessing a category* differ from *being able to classify into it*? The model can be said to have the operator categories in some sense — it distinguishes them substrate-invariantly on familiar words. But it cannot induct new instances by inferring category-membership from context. Is this category-possession, classifier-possession, or something between?

- **Information theory and coding.** A "structure that works perfectly on memorised instances but cannot generalise to novel ones" sounds like it might be describing a specific coding strategy — perhaps the model represents the trained vocabulary as a large set of dedicated per-word geometric attractors rather than as a region-and-class structure. Is there a clean information-theoretic framing for this distinction, and would it predict where else we should expect to see trained-vocabulary-bound substrate-invariance combined with novel-instance generalisation failure?

- **Evolutionary and developmental biology, morphospace** *(new since the last version)*. We borrowed the morphospace framing — what shapes can exist, and where the edges are — to ask the analogous question about representational shapes a neural network can compute. The result identifies a specific edge: the boundary between *trained-vocabulary substrate-invariance* (inside the edge — any in-vocabulary word transfers substrate-invariantly across notation changes) and *novel-operator generalisation* (outside the edge — novel operator words do not get routed into the right category). The edge is not a continuous gradient — it is sharp, in the sense that within-vocabulary behaviour passes all our cross-notation tests robustly and outside-vocabulary novel-operator behaviour fails them all. Are there biological morphospace results where one specific axis of variation is closed (no organisms in this direction) while a closely-related axis is open? The structural reason for the closure in our case — that the model has memorised per-word attractors but not a generative process for new ones — feels like it might have biological analogues.

## What we are doing next

The Phase 1 + Phase 2 picture is now stable across three model families. The pre-registered fourth-reading test confirmed the main result while surfacing a refinement of the default-routing mechanism; the fifth-reading causal-intervention test grounded the project's strongest positive finding and clarified what it does and does not say about the model's behaviour; the sixth-reading embedding-similarity test ruled out the most natural single-factor closing of the mechanism gap and identified *probe-decision-boundary geometry* as the residual unexplained component; the seventh-reading content-word control reframed the positive finding from the v1-draft "operator-set-bound" claim to the broader "trained-vocabulary-bound" claim. With those four follow-ups complete, the experimental programme is closed and the paper draft is being revised around the new framing.

Future directions, in priority order:

- **Novel-content-word generalisation test.** The seventh-reading control broadens the positive finding to "trained-vocabulary substrate-invariance" but leaves the negative finding (novel-instance generalisation failure) demonstrated only for novel *operator* words. The analog test — would novel content words inserted into a content-word readout exhibit the same default-to-rarest-canonical compression that novel operators do? — is now the natural single open empirical question. If novel content words also collapse to a default attractor, the negative finding generalises to "novel-word generalisation fails" across word classes; if they do not, the operator-vs-content asymmetry becomes a substantive part of the finding.

- **Sparse-autoencoder feature labelling.** The sixth-reading test identified *probe-decision-boundary geometry* as the residual unexplained mechanism component but did not characterise it. Sparse-autoencoder analysis at the relevant intermediate layers should decompose the classifier's discriminating direction into a small set of interpretable feature directions — most likely some combination of training-corpus frequency signals, subword-tokenization signals, and per-word residual-stream structure not captured by averaging. This would close the mechanism description rather than extending the scope.

- **Instruction-tuned variants.** All three models we have tested are pre-instruction-tuning base checkpoints. Whether instruction tuning expands the model's novel-operator-generalisation capability (partially refuting the negative finding), strengthens the trained-vocabulary geometric structure (strengthening the positive finding's causal grounding), or has no effect on either, is an open question with non-trivial implications for safety-relevant capability claims.

- **Structural-domain expansion.** Our entire programme is currently scoped to propositional-logic operators. Whether the trained-vocabulary-bound pattern (positive half) and the novel-operator generalisation failure (negative half) extend to set theory, simple type theory, finite-group arithmetic, or other formal-structure domains is the next major question for scope, and the natural follow-up to the cross-family replication phase.
